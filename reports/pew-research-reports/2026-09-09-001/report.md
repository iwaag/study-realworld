# Latinos and the American Dream: Split Views, but Optimism About Family Progress

**Source:** Pew Research Center, "Latinos Are Split on Whether the American
Dream Is Achievable," published September 1, 2026.
https://www.pewresearch.org/race-and-ethnicity/2026/09/01/latinos-are-split-on-whether-the-american-dream-is-achievable/

## Methodology

The report draws on Pew's National Survey of Latinos, fielded October 6-16,
2025, among 8,046 U.S. adults, including 4,923 Hispanic respondents (1,125
from Pew's American Trends Panel and 3,798 from the SSRS Opinion Panel).
The bilingual survey used random residential-address sampling with online or
phone interviews, weighted to represent the U.S. adult population.

## Key findings

Latino adults are divided on whether the American dream is still attainable.
About one in five (20%) say they have already achieved it, and a third (33%)
say they are on their way to achieving it. But a meaningful share is
skeptical: 18% say the dream is no longer attainable, and 27% say it never
existed in the first place. Compared with other Americans, Latinos are
somewhat less likely to say they've already achieved the dream (20% vs. 28%
among non-Hispanic adults), though similar shares of both groups say they are
working toward it.

Despite that skepticism about the dream in the abstract, Latinos report
notable optimism about their own family's trajectory. A majority (57%) say
their standard of living is better than their parents', more than double the
23% who say it is worse - a more positive intergenerational assessment than
non-Hispanic Americans give (only 44% of whom say they're better off than
their parents). Looking forward, 53% of Latino parents expect their children
will be financially better off than they are, versus just 14% who expect
their children to be worse off. Immigrant Latinos are consistently more
optimistic on these measures than U.S.-born Latinos.

When asked what the American dream actually requires, respondents pointed
overwhelmingly to individual effort and structural opportunity rather than
chance: 81% cited hard work and motivation as essential, 76% pointed to
having access to opportunity, and only 35% considered luck an important
factor. For most respondents, the dream itself is defined in concrete terms -
financial stability, homeownership, and a good quality of family life -
rather than as an abstract ideal.

## Takeaway

The report's central tension is that Latinos are more pessimistic than other
Americans about whether "the American dream" as a general concept is still
achievable, yet more optimistic than other Americans about their own
family's concrete economic progress across generations. Immigration status
(foreign-born vs. U.S.-born) is the strongest internal dividing line within
the Latino population on these questions.
